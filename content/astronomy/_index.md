<p class="astronomy-lead">I study high-energy and non-thermal astrophysical phenomena through theoretical modelling, numerical simulations, and multi-wavelength observations. My research has resulted in 60+ peer-reviewed publications, including 8 first-author papers. <a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/public-libraries/8vGw9SXzT7u1T1IWo1Ew6A">Full publication list on ADS</a>.</p>

<section class="astronomy-section"><p class="astronomy-eyebrow">How I work</p><h2>From physical questions to physical inference</h2><p>Numerical models motivate observations, observations constrain and refine the models, and comparison between them turns measurements into physical insight: physical question → model → prediction → proposal → data analysis → model comparison → inference.</p><div class="research-approach"><article><span>01</span><h3>Modelling</h3><p>Multi-zone models combine particle acceleration, radiative processes, transport, and source geometry to predict broadband emission.</p></article><article><span>02</span><h3>Observations</h3><p>Radio imaging, polarimetry, broadband SEDs, spectroscopy, and X-ray analysis connect those predictions with measured systems.</p></article></div></section>

<section class="astronomy-section astronomy-feature" id="massive-stars"><p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Research line 01</p><h2>Massive stars</h2><p>Powerful stellar winds, colliding winds, and stellar bow shocks create strong shocks where relativistic particles can be accelerated. This work follows a progression from modelling to prediction, observations, and physical constraints.</p><div class="project-story"><article><p class="card-kicker">Modelling</p><h3>A multi-zone model</h3><p>During my PhD, I developed a three-dimensional multi-zone numerical model for non-thermal emission from massive colliding-wind binaries. Including particle propagation and radiative effects, it became the backbone for subsequent predictions and observations.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201628264">del Palacio et al. 2016, A&amp;A 591, 139</a></p></article><article><p class="card-kicker">Prediction and observations</p><h3>HD 93129A</h3><p>For this extreme long-period (&gt;80 yr) binary, the model predicted enhanced emission near the 2020 periastron. I coordinated X-ray observations in a multi-frequency campaign, finding evidence for non-thermal X-rays and constraining particle acceleration and magnetic fields.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/staa1156">del Palacio et al. 2020, MNRAS 494, 6043</a></p></article><article><p class="card-kicker">Apep · NuSTAR</p><h3>Non-thermal X-rays</h3><p>Radio absorption and modelling of this peculiar Wolf-Rayet binary predicted detectable high-energy emission. I led the NuSTAR observations and radio/X-ray analysis, using the detected non-thermal component to constrain particle acceleration and magnetic fields.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202245505">del Palacio et al. 2023, A&amp;A 672, 109</a></p></article><article><p class="card-kicker">Apep · ALMA</p><h3>Resolving the stellar winds</h3><p>In a later, separate ALMA project, I am Principal Investigator of project 2024.1.00060.S, awarded 1.2 hours to characterise Apep's stellar winds at high angular resolution. These observations test the wind structure directly rather than the NuSTAR high-energy result.</p><p><a href="https://almascience.nrao.edu/aq/?projectCode=2024.1.00060.S">ALMA project 2024.1.00060.S</a></p></article></div><figure class="astronomy-figure"><img src="/images/Apep_X-ray_SED.png" alt="X-ray spectral energy distribution of the colliding-wind binary Apep"><figcaption>The Apep spectrum separates the dominant thermal emission below about 20 keV from the higher-energy power-law component attributed to inverse-Compton emission, providing evidence for non-thermal X-rays.</figcaption></figure></section>

<section class="astronomy-section bowshock-section"><div class="bowshock-copy"><p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Other research</p><h2>Particle acceleration in moving stellar systems</h2><p>Multi-zone models showed that bow shocks can accelerate particles despite inefficient high-energy radiation and helped identify promising targets. VLA polarimetry unexpectedly found low polarisation, challenging assumptions about magnetic-field ordering. Later modelling of observed bow shocks produced predictions relevant to SKA-related work.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202347720">Martínez et al. 2023, A&amp;A 680, 99</a></p></div><figure><img src="/images/bowshock_sketch.png" alt="Sketch of a stellar bow shock"><figcaption>A bow shock forms where a moving star’s wind meets the surrounding medium; the geometry sets the site and direction of particle acceleration.</figcaption></figure></section>

<section class="astronomy-section" id="pulsar-monitoring"><p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Research line 02</p><h2>Pulsar Monitoring in Argentina</h2><p>In 2017 I was one of the founding members of the Pulsar Monitoring in Argentina (PuMA) group. Together with technical staff, I helped turn two 30-m IAR antennas that had been largely inactive for more than a decade into a long-term programme, the first of its kind in South America.</p><div class="impact-grid"><div><strong>9</strong><span>refereed publications</span></div><div><strong>8</strong><span>Astronomer’s Telegrams</span></div><div><strong>6</strong><span>conference proceedings</span></div><div><strong>1</strong><span>programme built from scratch</span></div></div><p>I helped establish the programme’s scientific and technical foundations: acquiring new capabilities, implementing timing software, developing the data-reduction pipeline, designing schedules, and shaping its scientific strategy. PuMA supported international collaborations including LIGO/Virgo and led to the first M.Sc. and PhD theses on pulsar timing in Argentina; I supervised the M.Sc. thesis and co-supervised the PhD thesis.</p><figure class="astronomy-figure puma-figure"><img src="/images/puma_poster.jpg" alt="Santiago presenting a Pulsar Monitoring in Argentina poster"><figcaption>The PuMA programme connected a newly operational monitoring capability with student training, long-term timing data, and international scientific collaborations.</figcaption></figure></section>

<section class="astronomy-section" id="radio-quiet-agn"><p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Research line 03</p><h2>Radio-quiet AGN</h2><p>Compact radio and millimetre emission probes the hot corona around a supermassive black hole. I developed an open-source numerical SED modelling tool for radio-to-far-infrared observations, with self-consistent compact-corona emission and a framework for inferring physical parameters. The results indicate characteristic coronal sizes of about 200 gravitational radii and magnetic-field strengths of tens of gauss.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202554936">del Palacio et al. 2025, “Millimeter emission from supermassive black hole coronae”, A&amp;A 701, 41</a></p><figure class="astronomy-figure"><img src="/images/SED_IC4329A.png" alt="Radio-to-far-infrared spectral energy distribution model for IC 4329A"><figcaption>Broadband SED modelling of IC 4329A combines radio, millimetre, and infrared measurements to constrain emission from its compact corona.</figcaption></figure></section>

<section class="astronomy-section"><p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Research line 04</p><h2>Tidal Disruption Events</h2><p>When a star is disrupted by a supermassive black hole, the resulting flare can launch outflows and produce evolving non-thermal radio emission. I have contributed to the study of this time-dependent emission as a probe of the outflow and its interaction with the surrounding environment.</p><figure class="astronomy-figure"><img src="/images/TDE_SED-evolution.png" alt="Radio spectral energy distribution evolution of a tidal disruption event"><figcaption>The evolving radio SED tracks how a tidal-disruption outflow changes as it interacts with its environment.</figcaption></figure></section>
