<section class="astronomy-section astronomy-feature" id="massive-stars"><h2>Massive stars</h2><p>Powerful stellar winds, colliding winds, and stellar bow shocks create strong shocks where relativistic particles can be accelerated. This work follows a progression from modelling to prediction, observations, and physical constraints.</p><div class="astronomy-subsection"><h3>Colliding-wind binaries</h3>
  <p>My early work focused on HD 93129A, an extreme colliding-wind binary with a period longer than 80 years. I developed a three-dimensional multi-zone model for its non-thermal emission, including particle propagation and radiative effects, and used it to predict enhanced emission around the 2018 periastron (<a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2016A%26A...591A.139D/abstract">del Palacio et al. 2016</a>). These predictions motivated and guided an X-ray observing campaign in 2018. The resulting multi-frequency analysis found evidence for non-thermal X-ray emission and constrained particle acceleration and magnetic fields (<a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.494.6043D/abstract">del Palacio et al. 2020</a>).</p>
   <p>Apep is an exceptional Wolf-Rayet colliding-wind binary whose radio, X-ray, and millimetre emission provides complementary views of its stellar winds and particle acceleration. Radio modelling of its strongly absorbed emission constrained the wind properties and predicted detectable non-thermal X-rays (<a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022PASA...39....4D/abstract">del Palacio et al. 2022</a>). I subsequently led a NuSTAR observing campaign and the radio/X-ray analysis, detecting a non-thermal component and using it to constrain particle acceleration and magnetic fields (<a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A%26A...672A.109D/abstract">del Palacio et al. 2023</a>).
   </p><p>In a separate, recent ALMA campaign, I am Principal Investigator of project 2024.1.00060.S, awarded 1.2 hours to resolve Apep's stellar winds at high angular resolution. The resulting paper has been submitted.</p><figure class="astronomy-figure"><img src="/images/Apep_X-ray_SED.png" alt="X-ray spectral energy distribution of the colliding-wind binary Apep"><figcaption>The X-ray spectrum from Apep reveals a non-thermal tail, consistent with the radio-modelling predictions. Taken from del Palacio et al. 2023.</figcaption></figure></div>

<div class="astronomy-subsection bowshock-subsection"><h3>Stellar bow shocks</h3><div class="bowshock-section"><div class="bowshock-copy"><p>High-velocity stars move through the surrounding interstellar medium, and the interaction between their stellar winds and that medium forms a bow-shaped shock ahead of the star. These shocks can accelerate particles, even when the resulting high-energy radiation is faint. Multi-zone models helped identify promising targets (<a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A%26A...617A..13D/abstract">del Palacio et al. 2018</a>), while VLA polarimetry unexpectedly found low polarisation, challenging assumptions about magnetic-field ordering (<a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021MNRAS.503.2514B/abstract">Benaglia et al. 2021</a>). Later modelling of observed bow shocks produced predictions relevant to SKA-related work (<a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023A%26A...680A..99M/abstract">Martínez et al. 2023</a>). We are currently working on a Python package to interactively explore the radiation produced by stellar bow shocks in multiple bands (Martínez et al., coming soon).</p>
</div><figure><img src="/images/bowshock_sketch.png" alt="Sketch of a stellar bow shock"><figcaption>A high-velocity star’s wind collides with the surrounding medium, forming a bow shock where particles can be accelerated. Taken from del Palacio et al. 2018.</figcaption></figure></div></div>

<div class="astronomy-subsection"><h3>Stellar bubbles</h3><p>Massive stars can excavate large bubbles in the surrounding interstellar medium through their powerful winds. These wind-blown structures contain shocks where particles may be accelerated and produce non-thermal radiation. Using the upgraded Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (uGMRT), we detected non-thermal radio emission from a Wolf-Rayet stellar bubble, providing evidence that these systems can act as particle-acceleration sites (<a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2019ApJ...884L..49P/abstract">Prajapati et al. 2019</a>). Follow-up work extended this investigation to other sources (<a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.526..750S/abstract">Saha et al. 2023</a>; <a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2026A%26A...708A.310S/abstract">Saha et al. 2026</a>).</p></div></section>

<section class="astronomy-section" id="agn">
  <h2>Active Galactic Nuclei</h2>
  <p>Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) are luminous sources powered by accretion onto supermassive black holes at the centres of galaxies. I study their high-energy and radio-to-millimetre emission to probe the physics of accretion, outflows, and compact nuclear environments.</p>

  <div class="astronomy-subsection">
    <p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Radio-quiet AGN</p>
    <h3>Millimetre emission from AGN coronae</h3>
    <p>Compact radio and millimetre emission can probe the hot corona around a supermassive black hole. I developed an open-source numerical SED modelling tool for radio-to-far-infrared observations, with self-consistent compact-corona emission and a framework for inferring physical parameters. The results indicate characteristic coronal sizes of about 200 gravitational radii and magnetic-field strengths of tens of gauss. <a href="https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025A%26A...701A..41D/abstract">Paper link.</a></p>

      <figcaption>Broadband SED modelling of IC 4329A combines radio, millimetre, and infrared measurements to constrain emission from its compact corona. Adapted from del Palacio et al. 2025.</figcaption>
    </figure>
  </div>

  <div class="astronomy-subsection">
    <p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Tidal Disruption Events</p>
    <h3>Radio emission from stellar disruptions</h3>
    <p>When a star wanders too close to a supermassive black hole, the resulting tidal forces can disrupt it, leading to a sudden accretion process that converts gravitational energy into mechanical and electromagnetic energy, launching outflows and produce evolving non-thermal (radio) emission. I have contributed to the study of this time-dependent emission as a probe of the outflow and its interaction with the surrounding environment.</p>

  </div>
