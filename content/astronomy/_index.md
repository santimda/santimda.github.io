<h1>Astronomy &amp; Astrophysics</h1>
<p class="astronomy-lead">I study high-energy and non-thermal astrophysical phenomena through a combination of theoretical modelling, numerical simulations, and multi-wavelength observations.</p>
<p>My research focuses on how energetic particles are accelerated and how their radiation can be used to probe magnetic fields, particle populations, and the physical conditions of extreme astrophysical environments.</p>

<p class="astronomy-eyebrow">How I work</p><h2>From physical questions to physical inference</h2>
<p>Numerical models motivate observations, observations constrain and refine the models, and the comparison between them turns measurements into physical insight: physical question → numerical model → observational prediction → proposal → data analysis → model comparison → physical inference.</p>
<div class="research-approach">
<article><span>01</span><h3>Modelling</h3><p>Numerical models of non-thermal emission combine particle acceleration, radiative processes, transport, and source geometry to predict broadband emission.</p></article>
<article><span>02</span><h3>Observations</h3><p>Experience with ALMA, VLA/JVLA, NOEMA, NuSTAR, and Chandra includes radio imaging, polarimetry, broadband SEDs, and X-ray analysis.</p></article>

<section class="astronomy-section astronomy-feature" id="massive-stars">
<p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Research line 01</p><h2>Massive stars</h2>
<p>Powerful stellar winds, colliding winds, and stellar bow shocks create strong shocks where relativistic particles can be accelerated. Their non-thermal radiation provides diagnostics of particle acceleration and magnetic fields.</p>
<div class="project-story"><article><p class="card-kicker">Colliding-wind binaries</p><h3>A multi-zone model</h3><p>During my PhD, I developed a multi-zone numerical model for the non-thermal emission of massive colliding-wind binaries. The model includes three-dimensional propagation and radiative effects and became the backbone of subsequent research.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201628264">del Palacio et al. 2016, A&amp;A 591, 139</a></p></article><article><p class="card-kicker">HD 93129A</p><h3>Model → prediction → campaign → constraints</h3><p>For this extreme long-period (&gt;80 yr) colliding-wind binary, the model predicted enhanced emission near the 2020 periastron. I coordinated X-ray observations within a multi-frequency campaign, finding evidence for non-thermal X-ray emission and constraining particle acceleration and magnetic fields.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/staa1156">del Palacio et al. 2020, MNRAS 494, 6043</a></p></article><article><p class="card-kicker">Apep</p><h3>Radio absorption and non-thermal X-rays</h3><p>For this peculiar Wolf-Rayet colliding-wind binary, radio absorption and modelling were used to investigate stellar mass-loss and predict detectable non-thermal X-rays. I led NuSTAR observations and the radio/X-ray analysis, producing constraints on particle acceleration and magnetic fields.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202245505">del Palacio et al. 2023, A&amp;A 672, 109</a> · <a href="https://almascience.nrao.edu/aq/?projectCode=2024.1.00060.S">ALMA project 2024.1.00060.S</a></p></article></div>
<figure class="astronomy-figure"><img src="/images/Apep_X-ray_SED.png" alt="X-ray spectral energy distribution of the colliding-wind binary Apep"><figcaption>Detection of non-thermal X-rays from the colliding-wind binary Apep (del Palacio+ 2023). The fitted model includes a thermal component (which dominates up to ~20 keV) and a power-law component that corresponds to IC emission (relevant only at E &gt; 20 keV).</figcaption></figure>
</section>

<section class="astronomy-section bowshock-section">
<div class="bowshock-copy"><p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Stellar bow shocks</p><h2>Particle acceleration in moving stellar systems</h2><p>Multi-zone models showed that bow shocks can accelerate particles despite inefficient high-energy radiation and helped identify promising observational targets. VLA polarimetric observations unexpectedly found low polarisation, challenging assumptions about magnetic-field ordering. Later modelling of observed bow shocks produced predictions relevant to SKA.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202347720">Martínez et al. 2023, A&amp;A 680, 99</a></p></div>

</section>

<section class="astronomy-section" id="pulsar-monitoring"><p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Research line 02</p><h2>Pulsar Monitoring in Argentina</h2><p>In 2017 I was one of the founding members of the Pulsar Monitoring in Argentina (PuMA) group. Together with technical staff, I helped turn two 30-m IAR antennas that had been largely inactive for more than a decade into a long-term pulsar-monitoring programme, the first of its kind in South America.</p><div class="impact-grid"><div><strong>9</strong><span>refereed publications</span></div><div><strong>8</strong><span>Astronomer's Telegrams</span></div><div><strong>6</strong><span>conference proceedings</span></div><div><strong>1</strong><span>programme built from scratch</span></div></div><p>I helped acquire new scientific capabilities, implement standard pulsar-timing software, develop a dedicated data-reduction pipeline, design observation schedules, and shape the scientific strategy and operation. The programme also supported training, thesis supervision, and international collaborations including LIGO/Virgo. It also led to the first M.Sc. and PhD theses on pulsar timing in Argentina: the M.Sc. thesis was supervised by me, and the PhD thesis was co-supervised by me.</p><figure class="astronomy-figure puma-figure"><img src="/images/puma_poster.jpg" alt="Santiago presenting a Pulsar Monitoring in Argentina poster"><figcaption>Presenting a PuMA poster.</figcaption></figure></section>

<section class="astronomy-section" id="active-galactic-nuclei"><p class="astronomy-eyebrow">Research line 03</p><h2>Active Galactic Nuclei</h2><div class="agn-subsections"><article><h3>Radio-quiet AGN</h3><p>In radio-quiet active galaxies, compact radio and millimetre emission can probe the hot corona surrounding a supermassive black hole. I developed an open-source numerical SED modelling tool for radio-to-far-infrared continuum observations, with self-consistent compact-corona emission and a framework for inferring physical coronal parameters. The main results are characteristic coronal sizes of ~200 gravitational radii and magnetic-field strengths of tens of Gauss.</p><p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202554936">del Palacio et al. 2025, “Millimeter emission from supermassive black hole coronae”, A&amp;A 701, 41</a></p><figure class="astronomy-figure"><img src="/images/SED_IC4329A.png" alt="Radio-to-far-infrared spectral energy distribution model for IC 4329A"><figcaption>Radio-to-far-infrared spectral energy distribution model for IC 4329A.</figcaption></figure></article><article><h3>Tidal Disruption Events</h3><p>When a star is disrupted by a supermassive black hole, the resulting flare can launch outflows and produce evolving non-thermal radio emission. I have contributed to the study of this time-dependent emission as a probe of the outflow and its interaction with the surrounding environment.</p><figure class="astronomy-figure"><img src="/images/TDE_SED-evolution.png" alt="Radio spectral energy distribution evolution of a tidal disruption event"><figcaption>Radio SED evolution of a tidal disruption event (Wang+ Science).</figcaption></figure></article></div></section>
